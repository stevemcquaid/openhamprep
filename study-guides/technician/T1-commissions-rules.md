# T1 — Commission's Rules

**6 of your 35 exam questions · 6 groups (T1A–T1F) · 68 questions in the pool**

T1 is the regulatory subelement — who runs the Amateur Radio Service, what your license actually
lets you do, and the paperwork mechanics of getting and keeping it. Almost nothing here requires
reasoning; it is names, numbers, and timeframes straight out of Part 97. Because it is pure
memorization with no math and no diagrams, it is one of the highest-value subelements for a
brand-new licensee to lock down early — six questions for the price of reading one document
carefully.

Every question in the pool is printed below with its correct answer marked and its Part 97
citation. Read the concept notes first, then work the questions; the notes are written to make the
questions predictable rather than to be memorized on their own.

## T1A — Purpose and permissible use of the Amateur Radio Service; Operator/primary station license grant; Meanings of basic terms used in FCC rules; Interference; RACES rules; Phonetics; Frequency Coordinator; Beacon

*One exam question comes from this group. 11 questions in the pool.*

**Who and why.** The **FCC** regulates and enforces Part 97 — not the ARRL, not Homeland Security.
The Basis and Purpose section (§97.1) lists several goals; the pool question tests one specific
phrase — **advancing skills in the technical and communication phases of the radio art** — so
resist the tempting "all these choices are correct" distractor.

**Proof of license, not paperwork.** What legally proves your operator/primary license grant is
that **it appears in the FCC's ULS database** — a printed CSCE or an NCVEC email is not the grant
itself. Relatedly, official notice of a new license and call sign comes as an **email from the FCC
with a link to download the license grant**; the FCC does not mail printed licenses.

**Phonetics are encouraged, not required.** On phone emissions, using a standard phonetic alphabet
is **encouraged** — there is no rule forcing it, even for emergency traffic or foreign contacts.

**Beacons and frequency coordinators.** Automatically controlled propagation beacons on HF live in
one specific place for Technicians: **10 meters, 28.200–28.300 MHz** — not "a portion of every HF
Technician band." A **Volunteer Frequency Coordinator** — not an FCC office — recommends
repeater/auxiliary channels, and that coordinator is **selected by the local amateurs** whose
stations are eligible to be repeaters or auxiliary stations.

**Two definitions worth memorizing verbatim.** A **space station** is an amateur station located
**more than 50 km above Earth's surface** — altitude is the only test, not who built it or how many
people are aboard. A **RACES** control operator needs, beyond an FCC amateur license, **certification
of current enrollment by a civil defense organization** — not ARES membership, not an ARRL letter.

**What's actually prohibited.** Of the choices you'll see, only **willful or malicious
interference** is banned outright; international VHF+ contacts and digital third-party traffic are
both allowed.

## T1B — Frequency allocations; Emission modes; Spectrum sharing; Transmissions near band edges; Contacting the International Space Station; Power output

*One exam question comes from this group. 12 questions in the pool.*

**Technician HF is thin, VHF/UHF is generous.** Technicians get **phone privileges on 10 meters
only** among the HF bands — specifically **28.300–28.500 MHz** — and CW/data across the rest of the
Technician 10 m allocation. Nowhere else on HF do Technicians have phone. Above 30 MHz, digital
modes such as **FT8** are authorized on **10 meters, 6 meters, and 2 meters** — all three, so "all
these choices are correct" is the right answer whenever that combination appears.

**Landmark frequencies worth memorizing.** **52.525 MHz** sits in the 6-meter band; **146.52 MHz**
sits in the 2-meter band (the national FM simplex calling frequency, though the pool only tests the
band identification). SSB phone above 50 MHz is legal in **at least some segment of every one** of
those bands.

**CW-only segments are narrow slivers.** **50.0–50.1 MHz** (6 m) and **144.0–144.1 MHz** (2 m) are
CW-only — don't confuse these narrow CW sub-bands with the much wider phone/data allocations
surrounding them.

**Secondary status means coexistence, not exclusion.** Where the Amateur Service is secondary, US
amateurs may **encounter non-amateur (primary) stations** in that segment and must avoid
interfering with them — the service isn't banned there, it just yields.

**Why nobody transmits at the exact band edge.** Three reasons all apply simultaneously: **allowing
for calibration error**, **keeping modulation sidebands inside the band**, and **allowing for
transmitter frequency drift** — the pool question wants "all these choices are correct."

**Power — Technician HF is capped much lower than General's.** On their HF band segments,
Technicians are limited to **200 watts PEP output** — a fraction of General's 1500 W on the same
10-meter band. Above 30 MHz (VHF/UHF), that cap jumps to the standard **1500 watts PEP output**,
except where specific rules restrict it further. Finally, **any Technician-or-higher** licensee may
contact the ISS on VHF — no NASA approval or upgrade required, contrary to two tempting distractors.

## T1C — Licensing: classes, sequential and vanity call sign systems, places where the Amateur Radio Service is regulated by the FCC, name and address on FCC license database, term, renewal, grace period, maintaining mailing address; International communications

*One exam question comes from this group. 11 questions in the pool.*

**Three classes issued today.** The FCC currently issues only **Technician, General, and Amateur
Extra** licenses. Novice, Technician Plus, and Advanced are legacy classes that can still be
renewed but are no longer newly issued — a distractor combination worth recognizing on sight.

**Vanity call signs are open to everyone.** **Any licensed amateur** may apply for a vanity call
sign — there is no class restriction and no minimum years-licensed requirement, despite what two of
the distractors imply.

**International communications stay non-commercial.** FCC-licensed amateurs may exchange
international communications **incidental to the purposes of the Amateur Radio Service and remarks
of a personal character** — business communications remain off-limits even internationally.

**Keep the FCC able to reach you.** If the FCC cannot reach you by email (the license's contact
address), the consequence is **revocation of the station license or suspension of the operator
license** — a real, not hypothetical, penalty.

**The license lifecycle, three numbers to memorize.** A license term runs **10 years**. Renewal may
be requested up to **90 days** before expiration. If it lapses, there's a **2-year grace period**
during which you may hold the license for renewal purposes but — this is the trap — **you may not
transmit** until the renewal is actually granted; the grace period protects your right to renew, not
your right to operate. You may start transmitting on a brand-new license **as soon as the grant
appears in the FCC's license database** — not upon receiving a CSCE, and not upon receiving anything
in the mail.

**Maritime operation.** You may operate from a **US-documented vessel** in international waters
with the **master's permission** — any FCC amateur license covers this; no special maritime
endorsement exists.

**Call sign format.** Technician licenses can carry a **Group D** format call sign — a two-letter
prefix, single digit, three-letter suffix pattern (e.g., KF1XXX).

## T1D — Authorized and prohibited transmissions; Sale of equipment

*One exam question comes from this group. 12 questions in the pool.*

**Off-limits countries.** Communications are prohibited with any country whose administration has
notified the **ITU** — not the ARRL, not the IARU — that it objects to such communications. It's an
opt-out system administered at the international level.

**One-way transmissions: mostly banned, with named exceptions.** General **broadcasting** is
prohibited outright. The permitted one-way exceptions that show up elsewhere in the pool —
International Morse code practice, telecommand/telemetry, brief test transmissions — are the
narrow carve-outs; broadcasting itself is not one of them.

**Obscured messages are almost never allowed.** Transmitting messages encoded to hide their meaning
is permitted in exactly one case: **control commands to space stations or model craft** (i.e.,
telecommand). Every other circumstance is a "never."

**Music is nearly always banned too.** The single authorized exception is when it's **incidental to
an authorized retransmission of manned spacecraft communications** — not low power, not short
duration, not high frequency.

**Selling gear over the air is fine — running a business isn't.** You may notify other amateurs of
equipment for sale or trade **when doing so is not on a regular basis** — the moment it looks like a
retail operation, it's no longer permitted, regardless of who owns the equipment or whether a profit
is made.

**Indecent or obscene language is flatly prohibited** — no FCC or ITU "banned word list" exists;
the ban is general and unconditional.

**Auxiliary stations, by definition,** carry **one-way transmissions between a remote repeater
receiver and the main repeater transmitter** — a narrow, specific definition, not any backup radio
or contest-support link.

**Compensation is rare.** A control operator may be compensated only in a short, specific list —
the pool tests **classroom instruction at an educational institution** as a valid case. Employer
equipment sales and local-broadcast emergency-info gathering are not on that list.

**Broadcasting-support communications** are allowed only when **directly related to the immediate
safety of human life or protection of property** and no other means of communication is available —
not for general news gathering or commercial programming.

**FCC's definition of broadcasting:** transmissions **intended for reception by the general
public** — the two-way/one-way distinction and "any transmission" distractors are both wrong.

**Identification exception.** The only listed case where you may transmit without identifying is
when **transmitting signals to control model craft** — brief adjustment transmissions and low power
levels do not exempt you. On-air test transmissions, by contrast, **must** identify the transmitting
station.

## T1E — Control operator: eligibility, designating, privileges, duties, location, required; Control point; Control types: automatic, remote

*One exam question comes from this group. 11 questions in the pool.*

**A control operator is always required.** An amateur station may **never** transmit without a
control operator — automatic control (as in a repeater) still has one, it's just not physically
present at the transmitter.

**Who designates the control operator, and who it can be.** The **station licensee** designates
the control operator. For a station operating through an amateur satellite or space station,
**any amateur allowed to transmit on the satellite's uplink frequency** may serve as control
operator — there's no blanket Extra-class-only rule, contrary to two distractors.

**Privileges track the control operator, not the equipment or the premises.** The transmitting
frequency privileges of a station are determined by **the class of operator license held by the
control operator** — not by the radio's capabilities and not by the highest class licensee merely
present in the room.

**Control point vs. transmitter location — a key distinction.** The **control point** is the
**location at which the control operator function is performed** — this can be physically separate
from both the transmitting antenna and the transmitting apparatus itself (think: operating over the
internet).

**Technician into Extra segments.** Outside of an emergency, a Technician licensee may operate in
an Amateur Extra Class band segment **at no time** — not even if "designated" to do so by an Extra
licensee. Class privileges aren't transferable that way.

**Shared responsibility.** When the control operator isn't the station licensee, **both the control
operator and the station licensee** are responsible for proper operation — responsibility doesn't
shift entirely to one party.

**Automatic vs. remote control — don't mix them up.** **Automatic control** is exemplified by
**repeater operation** — no human control point is manned in real time. **Remote control** is
exemplified by **operating the station over the internet** — a human control operator is actively
involved, just not co-located with the transmitter. **Any station** may be remotely controlled;
there's no category exempted from it.

**Definition to memorize verbatim.** A control operator is **an amateur operator designated by the
licensee of a station to be responsible for transmissions and FCC rules compliance at that
station** — the emphasis is on "designated," which rules out the choice describing someone merely
identified in the FCC database.

## T1F — Station identification; Repeaters; Third party communications; Club stations; FCC inspection

*One exam question comes from this group. 11 questions in the pool.*

**FCC inspections happen on demand.** The station and its records must be made available **at any
time upon request by an FCC representative** — there is no advance-notice requirement and no
warrant requirement.

**Identification timing is consistent everywhere.** Whether you're using your assigned call sign
directly or a tactical call sign like "Race Headquarters," the rule is the same: identify with your
**FCC-assigned call sign at least every 10 minutes during and at the end of a communication** —
tactical calls never substitute for it.

**Identification language and method.** When using phone emissions, identification must be in
**English**, regardless of what language the rest of the contact used. Identification on a
phone-emission station may be sent using **either CW or phone emission** — not phone only.

**Self-assigned indicators are flexible in wording.** "Stroke," "slant," and "slash" before a
portable/mobile designator (e.g., "KL7CC stroke W3") are all acceptable — the words are
interchangeable, so "all these choices are correct" is the answer whenever this trio appears
together.

**Third-party traffic to a non-licensed speaker requires a treaty.** When a non-licensed person
speaks to a foreign amateur station through a station under the control of a licensed US amateur,
the restriction is that the **foreign station must be in a country with which the US has a
third-party agreement** — nationality of the speaker and who performs the ID are not the controlling
factors here.

**Two definitions to keep straight.** **Third-party communications** is a message from a control
operator to another station's control operator **on behalf of another person** — it has nothing to
do with three stations being on frequency together. A **repeater station** is one that
**simultaneously retransmits the signal of another amateur station on a different channel or
channels**.

**Repeater rule violations land on the originating operator.** If a repeater inadvertently
retransmits a rule-violating communication, accountability falls on **the control operator of the
originating station** — not the repeater's control operator or owner. This mirrors the same logic
you'll see for auxiliary and message-forwarding systems elsewhere in the pool: the person who
introduced the violating content is responsible, not the relay.

**Club stations need a minimum roster.** A club station license grant requires the club to have
**at least four members** — there's no requirement that the trustee hold Extra class or that the
club be registered with the ARRL.
