# G3 — Radio Wave Propagation

**3 of your 35 exam questions · 3 groups (G3A–G3C) · 37 questions in the pool**

The most genuinely interesting subelement, and the one where understanding beats memorizing by the
widest margin. Build the mental model of the ionosphere once and most answers fall out of it.

**The core model.** The Sun ionizes the upper atmosphere. Ionized gas refracts radio waves back
toward Earth — that is skip. Four regions, stacked bottom to top: **D** (absorbs below ~10 MHz,
daytime only), **E** (~1,200 miles per hop), **F1**, and **F2** (highest, ~2,500 miles per hop, the
workhorse of HF DX). More solar activity means more ionization, which means the higher bands open.

## G3A — Sunspots and solar radiation; geomagnetic field and stability indices

*One exam question comes from this group. 14 questions in the pool.*

**The indices.** Two pairs that get confused:

| Index | Measures |
|---|---|
| Sunspot number | Higher means better propagation at **higher** frequencies |
| **Solar flux index** | Solar radiation at a wavelength of **10.7 cm** |
| **K-index** | **Short-term** stability of **Earth's** geomagnetic field |
| **A-index** | **Long-term** stability of **Earth's** geomagnetic field |

Both K and A measure *Earth's* field, not the Sun's. The only difference is the time scale —
**K is Kwick**.

**Three different delays**, and this is the most-missed set in G3:

| Event | Time to reach us |
|---|---|
| Solar flare (UV and X-rays, at light speed) | **8 minutes** |
| Coronal mass ejection (particles, much slower) | **15 hours to several days** |
| Solar rotation cycle | **26–28 days** |

The 26–28 day cycle happens because **the Sun's surface layers rotate**, bringing the same active
regions back around.

**Disturbances.** A sudden ionospheric disturbance **hurts lower frequencies more**. A geomagnetic
storm is a **temporary disturbance in Earth's geomagnetic field** and it **degrades high-latitude HF
paths** — but its upside is **auroras that reflect VHF signals**. Coronal hole particles **disturb**
HF. At solar minimum, **15, 12, and 10 meters** are least reliable, while **20 meters works
worldwide in daylight at any point in the cycle**.

## G3B — Maximum and Lowest Usable Frequency; short and long path; ionospheric refraction

*One exam question comes from this group. 12 questions in the pool.*

**The window.** Two frequencies bracket what works on a path right now. The **MUF** is the maximum
usable frequency **between two specific points**; the **LUF** is the lowest. Both definitions are
point-to-point — "during a 24-hour period" is the distractor on both questions.

| Frequency | Result |
|---|---|
| Between LUF and MUF | **Refracted back to Earth** — it works |
| Above the MUF | Passes through into space |
| Below the LUF | **Attenuated** before arriving |
| **LUF above MUF** | **No skywave propagation possible on that path** |

**The best frequency to use is just below the MUF**, because that path takes the shallowest trip
through the absorbing lower layers.

**Determining conditions** practically: use a **network of automated receiving stations on the
internet** to see where your own transmissions land. Reverse beacon networks and PSK Reporter, not
the A-index and not listening for your own echoes.

**Hop distances: E region 1,200 miles, F2 region 2,500 miles.** Signals arriving by both short and
long path produce **a slightly delayed echo**. And the low bands in summer suffer from **high
atmospheric noise and static**.

## G3C — Ionospheric regions; critical angle and frequency; HF scatter; NVIS

*One exam question comes from this group. 11 questions in the pool.*

**Two "critical" terms, both meaning highest.** The **critical frequency** is the *highest* frequency
refracted back to Earth at a given angle. The **critical angle** is the *highest* takeoff angle that
returns a wave to Earth. If you remember that both are "highest," you get both questions even if you
mix up which is which.

**The D region explains the low bands.** It is the **closest region to Earth**, it is the **most
absorbent below 10 MHz during daylight**, and it is why **40, 60, 80, and 160 meters are hard in the
daytime**. It vanishes after dark, which is when those bands come alive.

**F2 gives the longest skip because it is the highest** — shallower geometry, longer hop. Not
density, not Doppler.

**Scatter** fills the skip zone, and every scatter question follows from it being multipath: signals
**flutter**, they sound **distorted because energy arrives by several different paths**, and they are
**weak because only a small part of the energy is scattered** in there.

**NVIS** is **short-distance MF or HF propagation at high elevation angles** — fire nearly straight
up, drop signals back down in a circle around you, fill in the skip zone. The matching antenna is in
G9D01.

## Bottom line for G3

**D absorbs, E hops 1,200 miles, F2 hops 2,500 miles and is highest.** **K is Kwick** (short-term),
A is long-term, both measure **Earth's** field. Solar flux is measured at **10.7 cm**. Flare **8
minutes**, CME **15 hours to days**, solar rotation **26-28 days**. Work **just below the MUF**.
**LUF above MUF means no path.** Both "critical" terms mean **highest**. **Scatter** fills the skip
zone, weak and fluttery. **NVIS** is high-angle short-range HF.
